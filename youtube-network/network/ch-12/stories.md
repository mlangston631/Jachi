# Crooked Arithmetic: stories, first five uploads, series, beat sheet for upload one

Builds on `market.md` and `cases.md` in this folder. Every fact below is tagged by what kind of fact it is:
**[conviction]**, **[plea]**, **[acquittal]**, **[court finding]**, **[settlement]** (no admission unless stated),
**[allegation]**, **[reported]** (credible journalism, not yet checked against a primary record) or **[verify]** (in
my notes or in one secondary source only; check it against a court record before scripting). No dialogue or quotation
is invented. The only quoted words are short phrases that the cited sources themselves report (for example, Petters
calling the purchase orders "fake" on the recordings) and have to be checked against the trial transcript or the
8th Circuit opinion before use. Nothing here is financial or legal advice.

### 1. ADM, the lysine cartel and the informant who was stealing (Mark Whitacre)

- **Title:** The FBI's Star Witness Was Stealing Millions
- **Alternatives:** "The Informant Who Taped a Price-Fixing Cartel for Three Years" / "Supermarket to the World: The
  Wiretap Inside ADM"
- **Thumbnail:** a cassette recorder on a corporate desk, the tape reel stamped with a lysine molecule; text
  "HE WORE THE WIRE". **Alt:** split frame, an FBI evidence tag on the left and an embezzlement cheque on the right;
  text "BOTH CRIMES".
- **Summary:** In 1992 a rising ADM executive, Mark Whitacre, became an FBI cooperator and recorded meetings in which
  ADM and Asian competitors fixed lysine prices. ADM pleaded guilty and paid a $100 million criminal fine (Oct 1996)
  **[plea]**; vice chairman Michael Andreas and Terrance Wilson were convicted (1998) and sent to prison (1999)
  **[conviction]** (US v. Andreas, N.D. Ill. 1998; 7th Cir. 2000). Then the twist: the informant had been
  embezzling from ADM himself (about $9 million to $9.5 million, by source) **[conviction]**. He pleaded guilty to fraud and tax charges and entered prison on 4 March 1998 with a nine-year
  sentence **[plea; verify the combined term with the price-fixing count]**, longer than the executives he helped
  convict. FBI and
  DOJ officials later questioned that length **[reported]**.
- **Central mystery:** How did the government's most valuable witness keep his own fraud hidden from the agents who
  spoke to him almost daily, and why?
- **Cold-open hook:** A corporate meeting room in the 1990s, recorded on the FBI's own equipment
  (public court-exhibit stills or audio only if released; otherwise a labelled recreation of the room). The man
  carrying the recorder is about to bring down one of America's biggest food companies, and he is the story's second
  criminal.
- **Turning points:** 1) An extortion claim brings the FBI to ADM; on 5 Nov 1992 Whitacre tells agent Brian Shepard about the price-fixing. 2) Years of recordings: the
  cartel meetings. 3) June 1995 FBI raid on ADM. 4) ADM tells prosecutors that Whitacre embezzled **[verify: in Eichenwald and press, not yet in a record I read]**.
  5) Whitacre's suicide attempt (reported by CBS; handle without detail). 6) ADM's guilty plea and $100M fine.
  7) Andreas and Wilson convicted. 8) Whitacre sentenced to more time than they were.
- **Why watch:** a double cross with no clean hero, a real recorded cartel, and a sentencing irony people argue about.
  Low YouTube coverage this year (best upload 883 views). The 2009 film *The Informant!* means the name is known but
  the record isn't.
- **Format:** two-part series (Series A below).
- **Key sources:** US v. Andreas (N.D. Ill. 1998); US v. Andreas and Wilson (7th Cir. 2000); ADM v. Whitacre
  (C.D. Ill. 1999); Whitacre post-conviction rulings (C.D. Ill. 2001, 2003); DOJ Antitrust Division 1996 release
  (ADM plea, $100M); Kurt Eichenwald, *The Informant* (2000); CBS News reports on the verdicts and on Whitacre's
  lawyer.

### 3. Equity Funding: the insurance company that invented its customers

- **Title:** The Insurance Company That Invented 64,000 People
- **Alternatives:** "The Whistleblower They Prosecuted Instead" / "The First Computer Fraud Was an Insurance Company"
- **Thumbnail:** a 1970s computer printout of policyholders, rows fading to blank; text "64,000 FAKE".
  **Alt:** the US Supreme Court facade with a 1973 stock ticker strip across it; text "HE TOLD THE TRUTH".
- **Summary:** Equity Funding Corporation of America (Los Angeles) inflated earnings and then, through its life
  insurance arm, created tens of thousands of fictitious policies (about 64,000 by most accounts **[reported]**) and
  sold them on to reinsurers. In March 1973 a fired executive, Ronald Secrist, told insurance analyst Raymond Dirks.
  Dirks investigated and told clients, some of whom sold, and pressed The Wall Street Journal and regulators. Trading
  was halted; bankruptcy followed on 5 April 1973 **[reported]**. Chairman Stanley Goldblum pleaded guilty and got
  eight years **[plea]**; 22 people pleaded guilty or were convicted **[reported]**. Then the twist: the SEC found
  Dirks had aided insider-trading violations and censured him **[verify: SEC administrative finding]**, and it took
  until 1983 for the Supreme Court to reverse (Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646) **[court finding]**.
- **Central mystery:** How do you hide people who don't exist from auditors, regulators and reinsurers, and why was
  the man who exposed it the one put on trial?
- **Cold-open hook:** March 1973. A stock analyst gets a phone call from a stranger who says a celebrated
  growth company is selling insurance on people who don't exist (narrated from the court record; no invented words).
- **Turning points:** funding-program origins; fake earnings first; the reinsurance trick; fabricated files; the fired
  employee; the analyst's investigation; trading halt; bankruptcy; guilty pleas; the SEC's case against Dirks; the
  Supreme Court.
- **Why watch:** a computer-age fraud before computers were everyday; a whistleblower punished; a landmark insider-trading
  case most viewers have never heard of. Best YouTube upload this year: 141 views.
- **Format:** standalone.
- **Key sources:** In re Equity Funding Corp. of America Securities Litigation (9th Cir. 1979; C.D. Cal. 1975, 1976,
  1977); In re Equity Funding Corp. of America Litigation (J.P.M.L. 1975); Equity Funding Corp. v. Loeffler (9th Cir.
  1975); Dirks v. SEC, 463 U.S. 646 (1983); SEC ALJ decision 1978 (sec.gov); Fortune archive "Those daring young con
  men of Equity Funding"; Ray Dirks and Gary Gentile's book *The Great Wall Street Scandal* (1974).

## The first five uploads, in order

1. **The Billionaire Whose Warehouses Were Empty** (Tom Petters). Standalone. The clearest promise of the channel:
   a huge number ($3.65B), a simple trick (forged purchase orders), an inside witness with a recorder, a 50-year
   sentence. It shows the format (following the money with documents on screen) with no prior knowledge needed.
   Ends with a natural pointer: "she wasn't the first insider to walk into the FBI with a recorder…"
2. **The Insurance Company That Invented 64,000 People** (Equity Funding). Standalone. Same skeleton (things that
   don't exist, sold as assets) but the 1970s and a whistleblower who gets punished. It proves the channel can make old
   records gripping and widens the audience past 2000s finance.
3. **The FBI's Star Witness Was Stealing Millions, Part 1: The Tapes** (ADM). The first series, released once 1 and
   2 have shown which thumbnail style works. The end of upload 1 already set up the "insider with a recorder".
4. **Part 2: The Informant's Ledger** (ADM), 7 days later. Pays off part 1's cliffhanger. The two-week arc gives
   new viewers a reason to subscribe rather than leave.
5. **The Bottled Water Fortune That Was 88% Fiction** (Le-Nature's). Back to a fast, visual standalone (gold,
   jewels, a product people know), with an ending (the commutation) that leads into discussion and comments.

Why this order builds a returning audience: uploads 1, 2 and 5 share one recognisable promise ("the assets didn't
exist, and here are the records that prove it"), so a viewer who liked one will click the next. Uploads 3 and 4 add
the series habit early (a two-parter with a real cliffhanger, then a payoff within a week), the one format that,
in the pairs in market.md, holds 60 to 90% of its audience when done well. The eras alternate (2008, 1973, 1990s,
2006) so the channel doesn't feel like one decade, and every film ends on a documented link to the next (a recorder,
an informant, an audit that missed it). All five have almost no competing YouTube coverage this year (fewer than
1,000 views on the best existing upload of each).
