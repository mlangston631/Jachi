## Title

**Chosen:** The $3.7 Billion Empire Built on Fake Purchase Orders

Alternatives:
1. She Went to the Authorities, Then Wore a Wire on Her Boss
2. The Electronics That Never Existed

Why: `verification.md` P1 to P3. No "billionaire" (no record says so) and no "empty warehouses" (no goods were ever
bought, so there was nothing to stock a warehouse with). $3.7 billion is the size of the Ponzi scheme in the DOJ
sentencing release of 8 April 2010, not a loss figure and not the value of any goods, so the title attaches it to the
empire, not to the electronics.

To follow what happened next, it helps to know one ordinary business document: the purchase order. A purchase

promised to buy a shipment, the risk in lending against that shipment looks small.

It is a real kind of business. Short-term financing for goods that are already sold is ordinary, and on paper it

### 11:01 Turning point

By the end of 2007, the arithmetic was turning against them. The government says the conspirators were struggling
to find new investors. And PCI was slow to pay hundreds of millions of dollars in promissory notes held by one of its
biggest sources of money, Lancelot Investment Management, which was run by a man named Greg Bell.

Petters told Bell the delay was the retailers' fault: they were paying him late. Bell agreed to extend the payments,
so the notes wouldn't go into default.

[VISUAL] [GFX] A promissory note whose due date is crossed out and moved forward.
[SOURCE] S1 ("Petters told Bell the slow payments were due to his retailers, who were late in paying him. As a

Then, in February 2008, according to the government, the two men went further. They agreed that Bell would take replacement purchase orders, from
other retailers, in place of the ones Lancelot already held. And Bell suggested they also swap money back and forth,
so it would look as if PCI was paying what it owed. Between late February 2008 and the day federal agents arrived,
the two men made more than eighty of these "round trip" transactions. Bell later pleaded guilty for his role.

[VISUAL] [GFX] Money arrows bounce between two boxes, "Lancelot" and "PCI", with a counter reaching "80+". Caption:
"'Round trip' transactions, Feb to Sept 2008 (DOJ)".
[SOURCE] S1 ("Bell suggested they also exchange money so it would appear that PCI was paying its notes"; "more than
80 'round trip' financial transactions"; Bell "already ha[s] pleaded guilty"); verification.md P16, P17.

A round trip, in this sense, is money that leaves and comes back. On a bank statement, a payment from PCI to its
lender looks exactly like a debt being paid. If the same money then travels back the other way, the debt hasn't
really shrunk at all. Only the appearance of it has. More than eighty times in about seven months is a sign of how
much appearance the scheme now needed.

[VISUAL] [GFX] One coin-shaped token travels from "PCI" to "Lender" and back, leaving a trail of "paid" stamps on a
ledger while the balance owed stays the same. Label "Illustration".
[SOURCE] S1 (round trips "intended to give the false impression that PCI was paying its obligations when due";
"Between late February 2008 and the date of the search warrants"). Explanatory illustration.

In this case, the last months didn't look like a crash. They looked like a frantic effort to make it appear that
nothing was wrong. And while that was happening, the one person who could see all of it made a decision.
